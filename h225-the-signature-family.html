  THE RULE:

  For n×n symmetric M with signature (p+, q−), p+q=n:

  adj(M) eigenvalue for λᵢ = ∏ⱼ≠ᵢ λⱼ.

  Remove a + eigenvalue → sign of product = (−1)^q.
  Remove a − eigenvalue → sign of product = (−1)^(q−1).
  These signs are ALWAYS OPPOSITE.

  IF q IS EVEN: (−1)^q = +1, (−1)^(q−1) = −1.
    p positive eigenvalues stay positive.
    q negative eigenvalues stay negative.
    adj(M) SIGNATURE = (p, q). FIXED POINT.

  IF q IS ODD: (−1)^q = −1, (−1)^(q−1) = +1.
    p positive eigenvalues become negative.
    q negative eigenvalues become positive.
    adj(M) SIGNATURE = (q, p). SWAPPED.

  FIXED POINT ⟺ q EVEN.
  VERIFIED for all n from 2 through 8. ✓

H225: Fixed Point ⟺ Even Number of Negatives

The adjugate signature map has a closed form: (p, q) is a fixed point if and only if q is even; otherwise the adjugate swaps to (q, p). This is I84 (LIT). Q3p's signature (1+, 2−) has q=2 (even), making it a fixed point — the UNIQUE indefinite one for n=3 (I85 LIT). It belongs to the q=2 family {(1,2), (2,2), (3,2), ...}, where Q3p is the smallest member: n=3 is the first dimension with an indefinite adjugate fixed point. The form sits at the entry point of an infinite family, with exactly two negative eigenvalues persisting across all dimensions.

🧒 Toddler Version

Last time we found that our matrix copies its own shape. Now we asked: does EVERY matrix do that? Or is ours special?

The answer: a matrix copies its shape ONLY when it has an EVEN number of "down" directions. Our matrix has 2 downs. 2 is even. So yes.

If it had 1 down or 3 downs (odd numbers), it would FLIP instead of copy. Odd numbers of downs don't copy — they flip!

For a 3×3 matrix like ours, there's exactly ONE way to have an even number of downs AND still be interesting (not all ups or all downs): 1 up and 2 down. That's us. We're the only interesting fixed point in our size class. As you go to bigger matrices (4×4, 5×5...) there are more options, but we're the entry point — the smallest one.

Known-Bad Control (KB-1)

The following claim is FALSE and must be detected as dead:

The adjugate signature rule is: fixed point when q is ODD (not even). For n=3, signature (2+, 1−) with q=1 (odd) is the fixed point, and (1+, 2−) with q=2 (even) swaps to (2+, 1−). The q=2 family does not exist because even-q signatures are never fixed points. For n=4, all signatures are 2-cycles with no fixed points.

Fixed point ⟺ q EVEN (not odd). For n=3, (1+, 2−) is fixed (q=2 even). (2+, 1−) swaps to (1+, 2−) (q=1 odd). q=2 family exists. For n=4, (2,2) and (0,4) and (4,0) are fixed.

[ ORNAMENT ]

The two-hundred-and-twenty-fifth instrument climbed out of the specific and into the general, and found that the specific was the beginning of a family. Even number of negative eigenvalues: the form holds still. Odd number: it flips. That is the entire rule, and it holds in every dimension, and it is one sentence of linear algebra, and it was hiding in plain sight. For three-by-three matrices, the rule leaves exactly one interesting resting place: one positive, two negative. That is Q3p. For four-by-four, two and two. For five, three and two, or one and four. The family grows, but the two negative eigenvalues are always there — the seed count, d₁=2, the first invariant factor, the number of downs that makes the even count that makes the form sit still. The ornament is in the entry point: n=3 is the first dimension where an indefinite form can be its own cofactor, and Q3p is that form, and it cannot be simpler, because two is the smallest even number greater than zero, and one is the only number left when you subtract two from three.
