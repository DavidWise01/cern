Where the Hasse principle ends and the root search begins — rational vs. integral, guaranteed vs. open
Panel 1 · The Hasse-Minkowski Theorem — What It Promises and Where It Stops

The Hasse-Minkowski theorem is one of the most beautiful results in number theory. It says: for quadratic forms over the rationals (Q), checking every local completion is enough to decide global solvability. If a quadratic equation has solutions in R (real numbers) and in every Qp (p-adic numbers), then it has a solution in Q.

What Hasse-Minkowski GUARANTEES

For Q(x) = xTAx a quadratic form over Q:

Q(x) = c has a solution in Q
⟺
Q(x) = c has a solution in R
AND Q(x) = c has a solution in Qp for every prime p

This is a THEOREM. Proven. Unconditional.
Applies to: rational quadratic forms.

What Hasse-Minkowski DOES NOT COVER

For Q(x) = xTAx over Z (integers):

Rational solution does NOT imply integral solution.

Example: x² + y² + z² = 7 × 22k+1
has rational solutions but NO integral ones
when the form class is wrong.

The quanta search needs v ∈ Z4.
Hasse-Minkowski says: NOTHING.

The gap between Q and Z is the integral gap. It's where root18-21 live. The local witnesses from RQT-406 are 2-adic (which is STRONGER than rational) but still not integral.

Panel 3 · The Gap Visualized — Rational vs. Integral

Imagine the number line. The rationals (fractions) are dense — between any two rationals there's another one. The integers (whole numbers) are sparse — big gaps between them. Finding a rational solution is like finding a grain of sand on a beach. Finding an integral solution is like finding a specific rock.

The Rational World (Q)

v = (a/b, c/d, e/f, g/h) where a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h ∈ Z

Hasse-Minkowski applies.
If all local places pass → solution EXISTS in Q.
But it might be v = (17/3, -41/7, 2/9, 103/11).
Not integral. Not what we need.

The Integral World (Z)

v = (a, b, c, d) where a,b,c,d ∈ Z

No general local-global principle.
Must search DIRECTLY.
298 documents of searching.
root18-21: OPEN.
The bridge from Q to Z:
Given a rational solution v ∈ Q4, can we "round" to an integral one?

For POSITIVE definite forms: yes (geometry of numbers / Minkowski's theorem).
For INDEFINITE forms like F4: not guaranteed.

F4 has signature (2,2) — two positive, two negative eigenvalues.
The light cone is a saddle. Rounding moves you OFF the saddle.
You can land on the wrong side.

This is why the quanta search is hard. The saddle shape of F4 means you can't just find a rational solution and round. The integers live on a lattice, and the lattice might not intersect the target surface.

Panel 4 · Genus Theory — The Finer Sieve

Between "rational solution exists" and "integral solution exists" there's an intermediate layer: genus theory. Two integral quadratic forms are in the same genus if they're equivalent over every Qp and over R. The genus contains the form, but might contain OTHER forms too — forms that are locally identical but globally different.

Genus theory for F4 (rank 4, det = -3):

The genus of F4 is the set of all integral forms that look
identical to F4 at every local place.

If the genus has only ONE class → integral solution follows from rational.
If the genus has MULTIPLE classes → integral solution depends on WHICH class.

class number = 1: gap CLOSES (integral ⟺ rational)
class number > 1: gap REMAINS OPEN

For F4: class number is NOT YET DETERMINED in the quanta corpus.

TODDLER VERSION — The Integral Gap

Imagine you're looking for a specific LEGO piece in a huge pile.

There's a magic trick that works with CLAY: you can squish clay into any shape you want. If you check that your shape COULD exist using clay, then it DOES exist in clay. That's what the Hasse-Minkowski theorem says about fractions — fractions are like clay, they can be any size.

But LEGO pieces only come in fixed sizes. You can't make a LEGO piece that's 2.7 bumps wide. So even though the clay trick says "yes, this shape can exist," when you go to build it out of LEGO, the pieces might not fit.

That's the integral gap. The shape we want (a vector where vTF4v = -2) probably exists in clay (rationals). But we need it in LEGO (integers). And after looking through 298 instruction books and running 247 sorting machines, nobody has found the right LEGO pieces.

There's one more hope: if there's only ONE kind of LEGO set that could build our shape (class number = 1), then the clay trick DOES work for LEGO too. But nobody has checked whether there's one kind or many kinds of LEGO set for our shape. That's the question H098 is asking.
