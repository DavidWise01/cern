H210: The Lock Makes Things Non-Practical

Stewart's criterion explains the 9/15 practical rate: the practical Rosetta members all have 2 as smallest prime factor with subsequent primes within the Stewart bound. The {2,3,7} factorization (shared by 42, 84, 336) forms a cascading permission chain. The lock family {17, 25, 289} is entirely non-practical — the lock's prime nature (17) propagates through escape (25=5²) and lock² (289=17²). Key insight: multiplication preserves practicality (the multiplicative core from H207 is entirely practical), while addition breaks it (the additive extensions introduce non-practical numbers). 336 has the most divisors (20). 🧒 Toddler Version

A "practical" number is one where you can make EVERY smaller number by adding up its parts (divisors). Like 6: its parts are 1, 2, 3, 6. And 1=1, 2=2, 3=3, 4=1+3, 5=2+3. You can make everything up to 6!

9 of our 15 special numbers are practical. WHY? Because they all have 2 as a building block. The number 2 is the golden ticket — if your smallest part is 2, you have a good shot at being practical.

The 6 that AREN'T practical? Five are odd (no factor of 2 at all), and the last one (10) has 2 but its next part (5) is too big of a jump.

The lock number (17) is especially bad — it's a lonely prime with no parts besides 1 and itself. Everything built from the lock (25, 289) inherits its loneliness. The lock family is the impractical family.

Known-Bad Control (KB-1)

The following claim is FALSE and must be detected as dead:

All 15 Rosetta numbers are practical, with zero failures, because Stewart's criterion is automatically satisfied whenever a number appears in a ternary quadratic form's invariant structure. The lock number 17 is practical because 17 is prime and all primes are practical by definition. The number 336 has only 4 divisors.

6 of 15 are NOT practical. Stewart's criterion is a number-theoretic property unrelated to quadratic forms. 17 is NOT practical (primes > 2 are never practical: can't make 2 from {1, p}). 336 has 20 divisors, not 4.
