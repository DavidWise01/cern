WHAT THE 3D SCENE SHOWS

Each particle's Compton sphere is tiled with Planck-area cells. The number of cells = the holographic bit count.

Z°: λ ≈ 2.16×10-18 m → ~5.6×1034 bits

J/ψ: λ ≈ 6.37×10-17 m → ~4.9×1037 bits

electron: λ ≈ 3.86×10-13 m → ~1.8×1045 bits

HEAVIER = SMALLER Compton sphere = FEWER holographic bits.

This is the holographic paradox: a heavier particle looks like it carries LESS boundary information.

Resolution: at black-hole scale, the Schwarzschild radius grows linearly with M while Compton shrinks as 1/M. They cross at MP. Below Planck mass: Compton wins (particle). Above: Schwarzschild wins (black hole).

TODDLER CORNER

Imagine you have a ball. How much stuff could you write on the OUTSIDE of the ball with a really tiny pen? That's the holographic principle — the maximum information in any ball-shaped region of space depends on the SIZE OF ITS SURFACE, not how much room is inside.

Now imagine the tiniest possible pen nib — it's the Planck length, about 0.000000000000000000000000000000000016 meters across. Each dot that pen makes is one "bit" of information.

A particle like the Z boson (from the atom-smasher data) has a little quantum "fuzzy ball" around it called the Compton wavelength. We count how many Planck dots fit on that ball's surface. For the Z: about 56,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 dots. That's 5.6 × 1034.

Weird part: heavier particles have SMALLER fuzzy balls, so they fit FEWER dots. Light things are holographically richer than heavy things. That stops making sense at the Planck mass, where the fuzzy ball equals the black-hole size. That crossing point is where our physics breaks.
