But does this enormous cross-coupling actually ROTATE the eigenvectors? A big force on a stiff structure barely moves it. How stiff is this structure?

We diagonalise the 2 by 2 effective Schur complement matrix. The eigenvectors of S tell us the EFFECTIVE collapse and stability directions — including the torroid's indirect influence. The angle between these and the original H122 eigenvectors is the answer.

Panel 9 — Toddler Version

Remember the bodyguard elephant sitting on the Pringle chip?

In H124, we found it creates a big invisible rope between the left side and the right side of the Pringle — a 981-digit rope. That is a VERY strong rope.

Now we asked: does the rope TWIST the Pringle? Like, does it pull the left side toward the right side?

Answer: the rope is strong, but the Pringle is STIFFER. The Pringle is 5 digits stiffer than the rope is strong. So the rope twists the Pringle by 0.002 degrees. That is about the width of a human hair seen from across a football field.

The collapse direction (the thin weak spot) is EXACTLY where it was before the elephant sat down. The elephant has not moved it at all.

The elephant is a PHANTOM. Big, heavy, strong — and completely invisible to the shape of the Pringle.

IMPROVEMENT RECOMMENDATION → H126: The Stiffness Tensor


H125 found that the eigenvalue gap (5 digits: 986d vs 981d) is the STIFFNESS that absorbs the 981-digit cross-coupling. But we have been working in the rotated {v1, v2, e3} basis where the gap looks like a simple 5-digit difference.


H126 would compute the CONDITION NUMBER of the effective Schur complement — the ratio of the largest to smallest eigenvalue magnitude. A large condition number means the matrix is nearly singular (nearly degenerate). How does the torroid change the condition number compared to the original 2 by 2 restriction? If the condition number barely changes, the stiffness is intrinsic. If it changes significantly, the torroid is modulating the near-degeneracy even though it cannot rotate the eigenvectors.
