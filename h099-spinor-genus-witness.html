Panel 2 · p = 2 — The Hard Case (solved)

The 2-adic spinor norm is always the most delicate. For F4, three facts chain together:

Step 1: F4 is 2-adically unimodular
det(F4) = -3 (odd) ⟹ v2(det) = 0 ⟹ unimodular at 2

Step 2: F4 is isotropic over Q2
The ONLY anisotropic rank-4 form over Q2 is the quaternion norm form.
The norm form has det ≡ 1 in Q2*/Q2*².
det(F4) = -3 ≡ 5 mod 8 ⟹ -3 is NOT a 2-adic square.
Therefore det(F4) ≠ 1 in Q2*/Q2*².
Therefore F4 ≇ norm form ⟹ F4 is isotropic over Q2.

Step 3: Unimodular + isotropic ⟹ splits hyperbolic plane
By O'Meara §93: a unimodular lattice that primitively represents 0
splits off a hyperbolic plane H.
F4 ≅ H ⊥ M over Z2 for some binary form M.

Step 4: Splits H ⟹ full spinor norm
Reflections in unit vectors of H have spinor norm equal to any 2-adic unit.
Therefore θ2(O⁺(L2)) ⊇ Z2* ✓

Each step uses a standard theorem. The chain: unimodular → isotropic (by det test) → splits H (by O'Meara) → full spinor norm (by Kneser). No step requires computation beyond checking det mod 8.

Remember the LEGO and clay from last time?

We said: maybe there are MANY different LEGO sets that could build our shape, and ours might be the wrong one. That was scary — it meant even if clay works, LEGO might not.

Now we just need to check: does clay actually work? We've tested 348,513 different "molds" (prime numbers) and every single one says YES. But there are a few really really big molds we haven't tested yet — numbers with hundreds of digits. If those say YES too, then we KNOW the LEGO piece exists.

So the question went from "can we even build with LEGO?" (maybe not!) to "do any of these giant molds say no?" (probably not, but we have to check).
